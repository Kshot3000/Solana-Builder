129. DLMM composition fee planner - the imbalanced part of a deposit pays like a swap, but floored

Tool 123 prices a DLMM swap's fee and states the composition fee as not modelled. This prices it, in the program's own compute_composition_fee. Adding liquidity into a DLMM pool's active bin is meant to match the bin's current token ratio; the part of a deposit that does not fit that ratio - the imbalanced amount, or delta - has economically swapped against the bin's own inventory, so the program charges it a fee at the pool's total fee rate (Tool 123's base plus variable rate, in billionths at a precision of 1,000,000,000, capped at 100,000,000): composition fee = floor(delta × total rate × (1,000,000,000 + total rate) ÷ 1,000,000,0002). Two choices in that formula are the lesson. The fee is grossed up by (1 + rate) rather than divided by (precision − rate) the way Tool 123's swap fee is, and it is floored at the smallest unit where the swap fee is ceilinged - so a composition fee never exceeds the equivalent swap fee on the same amount at the same rate, and at dust scale it can vanish entirely. At the headline settings - Tool 123's pool (a 25 bps bin step, a base factor of 4,000, an accumulator of 100,000 and a control of 4,000, for a total rate of 1,250,000 (12.5 bps)) - an imbalance of 2,000 pays a composition fee of 2.503125 (12 bps of the imbalance, floored), so 1,997.496875 of it lands in the bin, while swapping the same 2,000 on top would pay 2.503128912 - a shortfall of 0.000003912 the floored formula leaves uncharged. At the program's capped 10% rate the same imbalance pays exactly 220 (1,100 bps of it - the rate times one plus the rate) against a swap fee of 222.222222223. The floor is the story at dust scale: a one-smallest-unit imbalance at the headline rate pays nothing at all, where the equivalent swap still charges its ceilinged full unit. A balanced deposit - an imbalance of zero - pays no composition fee at any rate, and a pool whose total rate is zero charges nothing. The deposit's split into the part that fits the bin's ratio and the imbalance (which follows the bin's live inventory and price), deposits into non-active bins, the fee's split between the protocol and the bin's providers, and the accumulator's decay over time are not modelled; the imbalanced amount is entered directly. Pool parameters and the amount are user-entered; no live pool is read. An educational model of a DLMM composition fee, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

128. NFT AMM pool planner - the exponential curve reprices every trade

Tool 119 sweeps a marketplace's resting listings cheapest-first; a pool has no listings at all. On Tensor's AMM - the NFT AMM pattern Solana's NFT liquidity runs on, modelled here from the tensor-foundation/amm program's own state code - a two-sided trade pool holds NFTs and SOL and prices every trade off three numbers: a starting price, a curve delta and a price offset that counts net taker buys (starting at 0, +1 per taker buy, -1 per taker sell). On an exponential curve the price at offset k is starting price × (1 + delta)k, delta entered in basis points and capped at 9,999 bps by the program, and each price is rounded the pool's way - up for a taker buy, down for a taker sell - so the pool's price walks away from anyone trading repeatedly in one direction. A trade pool's sells are priced one step lower, at offset - 1 - the program's own guard against draining a pool with matched buys and sells - and the market-maker fee, floor(price × fee / 10,000) per trade and capped at 7,500 bps, is added to what a buyer pays and taken from what a seller receives. At the headline settings - a 10 starting price, a 1,000 bps (10%) delta, offset 0 and a 500 bps fee - buying 3 costs 10, 11 and 12.1, a gross 33.1 and 34.755 all-in, with the next buy priced at 13.31; selling 3 back starts one step down at 9.09090909 and grosses only 24.868519908, 23.625093914 after fees. One matched round trip at the headline - buy one at 10, sell it straight back - loses 1.863636364 (1,774 bps of the 10.5 paid all-in): the pool's 909 bps pre-fee spread plus the fee charged on both sides. The same pool started at offset -2 (more sells than buys so far) prices its next buy at 8.26446281, and at a zero delta the curve is flat - every price is the starting price, the spread is zero and a round trip loses exactly the two fees. At dust scale the rounding is the story: a 3-smallest-unit starting price at a 9,999 bps delta sells its second NFT for 0, the floored price of a unit the curve has divided away. The linear curve, one-sided NFT and token pools (which charge no market-maker fee), creator royalties, Tensor's own taker fee and the pool's SOL and NFT balances are not modelled, and a price the curve would push past the u64 lamport ceiling the program's checked math enforces is rejected rather than displayed. 127. Bridge guardian quorum planner - two-thirds plus one, or the message does not exist

Tool 118 prices what a bridge charges; this prices what a bridge message must prove. In the Wormhole pattern - the bridge Solana's ecosystem grew up with - a set of guardians each independently observes a message on the source chain and signs it, and the destination chain accepts the resulting verified action approval (VAA) only once enough distinct guardians have signed. The threshold is the protocol's own formula (CalculateQuorum in Wormhole's SDK, written to match its contracts including Solana's bridge program): quorum = floor(2n / 3) + 1 for a set of n guardians. The +1 is load-bearing exactly when 3 divides n: two-thirds of 6 is exactly 4, but the quorum is 5, and a 3-guardian set needs all 3 - the rule demands strictly more than two-thirds, never exactly two-thirds. At the headline settings - a set of 19 guardians with 12 distinct signatures collected - the quorum is 13 (6,842 bps of the set); the 12 signatures are 6,315 bps of the set and the message is 1 short, so no VAA exists and nothing can be delivered - below quorum there is no partial delivery. Two tolerances fall out of the same number: 6 of the 19 guardians can be offline while the rest still reach quorum, and 7 refusing to sign block every message - a liveness failure, not a forgery, because producing a false VAA takes the full quorum itself, 13 compromised guardians. Signatures past quorum are surplus and add nothing: all 19 signing leaves a surplus of 6. A delegated subset runs the same formula at its own size - 7 guardians need 5, with an offline tolerance of 2 and a blocking set of 3 - and the largest set the format carries is 255, because a VAA counts its signatures in one byte. Signatures are counted as distinct and assumed valid; signature verification itself, guardian-set rotation and governance, the message payload and the bridge's transfer fees (Tool 118) are not modelled. Counts are user-entered; no live bridge or guardian set is read. An educational model of a bridge guardian quorum, not a live bridge. Not financial advice.

113. Delta-neutral basis (cash-and-carry) planner - the trade where the price cancels and funding is the return

107. Perp insurance fund & socialized-loss planner - who pays when a liquidation falls short

105. Flash loan repayment & profit planner - the loan that must repay inside its own transaction, or never settle

99. Vault share planner - donations, rounding and the first-depositor trap

At the headline settings - a 100 bps step from bin 0 (price exactly 1), 1,000 of the received token per bin - a 2,500 swap drains bins 0 and 1 for 1,000 + 1,010, spends its last 490 in bin 2 at 1.0201 and receives 480.345064209 there: 2,480.345064209 in total at an average price of 1.007924274. The walk is honest about running out: over only 2 bins, a 100,000 swap spends 2,010 and leaves 97,990 unfilled rather than filling at a made-up price. Every bin here holds the same available output (a user entry) - real bins hold uneven amounts, and the swap fee (a base fee plus the venue's variable volatility fee) is priced by Tool 123. An educational model of a DLMM bin walk, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

90. Aggregator split-route planner - one swap, split across two pools

An aggregator does not have to send a whole swap to one pool: it can split the input across two pools for the same pair in parallel and add the outputs - the core mechanic behind Jupiter-style routing. Each leg is priced by its own pool's curve and fee tier (Tool 2), the split is a whole percentage of the input to pool 1 floored at the smallest unit, and pool 2 receives the remainder, so the legs' inputs sum to the amount in exactly. At the headline settings - two identical 1,000,000 / 1,000,000 pools at 25 bps, 100,000 in, split 50% - each leg returns 47,505.65543517, a total of 95,011.31087034 against 90,702.432370993 from either pool alone: a gain of 4,308.878499347 (475 bps), because price impact is convex and two half-trades each move their pool half as far. Splitting is not automatically better: send half into a much shallower second pool and the split returns less than the best single pool, which is why a real aggregator searches pools and split sizes instead of splitting blindly. Reserves, amounts, fees and the split are user-entered. An educational model of split routing, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

89. Platform-fee swap planner - the interface's cut, taken from what you receive

A swap interface can charge its own fee on top of the pool's: the integrator names a rate in basis points and the fee is taken from the output of the swap - the gross output the pool's curve returns (Tool 2) is reduced before the user is credited - then split with a referrer in whole percentage points, the referrer's cut floored and the platform keeping the remainder, so the parts sum to the fee exactly. At the headline settings - a 1,000,000 / 1,000,000 pool, 10,000 in, a 25 bps pool fee and a 20 bps platform fee split 50% to a referrer - the gross output is 9,876.48209114, the platform fee withholds 19.752964182 (9.876482091 to the referrer, 9.876482091 kept), and the user receives 9,856.729126958: a total drag of 143 bps against the 10,000 spot output. Because the fee is charged on the output it never enters the curve and does not move the price - it only shrinks what arrives; on a dust output it can floor to exactly zero. The platform rate here is capped at 1,000 bps as a sanity bound; real integrator fees are far below it. Reserves, amounts and rates are user-entered. An educational model of platform-fee swaps, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

88. Validator net-economics planner - commission in, vote costs out

Tools 79 and 87 priced the two sides of a validator's staking ledger separately; this tool settles them. The income side is commission: the vote account's whole-percentage cut of the rewards its total delegated stake earns at an assumed gross rate. The cost side is voting: 5,000 lamports per vote, one expected per slot, the same for every stake. At 50,000 SOL delegated, a 700 bps gross rate and a 5% commission, one 432,000-slot epoch earns 19.178082191 SOL of rewards and the commission keeps 0.958904109 SOL - against a vote cost of 2.052 SOL for voting on 95% of slots, a net of -1.093095891 SOL per epoch (-10.93095891 over 10 epochs). The break-even delegated stake is 106,997.142857143 SOL: the smallest stake whose floored commission covers the vote cost, with one smallest unit less still falling one lamport short. At 500,000 SOL the same settings net +7.537041095 SOL per epoch. At a 0% commission (or a 0% gross rate) no stake breaks even, and a voted share that floors below one vote costs nothing. Block revenue and MEV commissions, hardware and other running costs, and rewards on a validator's own stake beyond its commission are not modelled. An educational model of validator net economics, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

87. Validator vote-cost planner - voting is a fee, and it is the same for every stake

A Solana validator takes part in consensus by voting, and a vote is an ordinary on-chain transaction: privileged, carrying exactly one signature, charged the 5,000-lamport base fee (Tool 5) and no priority fee. One vote is expected per slot, so an epoch's vote cost is just votes × 5,000 - and it never scales with stake. Voting on 95% of a 432,000-slot epoch casts 410,400 votes and costs 2,052,000,000 lamports (2.052 SOL) per epoch - 1.026 SOL per nominal day at 400-millisecond slots - against 2.16 SOL for voting on every slot, the full-participation rate behind the official validator FAQ's up to 1.1 SOL per day figure. Over 10 epochs the 95% plan pays 20.52 SOL. The only saving is a slot not voted on, and that slot also earns no vote credit toward the epoch's inflation rewards. A validator with 100 SOL delegated pays exactly what a 1,000,000 SOL validator pays, which is why vote costs are the fixed hurdle every small validator must clear - and commission (Tool 79) is the income side of that same ledger. Slots, voted share and epochs are user-entered. An educational model of validator vote costs, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

86. Nakamoto coefficient planner - how few validators can stall finality

Tool 84 priced the line - finality stalls once a third of the activated stake is offline - and this tool asks who can reach it. Rank the validators largest first and walk down until the running sum reaches the halt line of 133,333,333.333333334 SOL on a 400,000,000 SOL total: entered stakes of 30, 25, 22, 20, 18, 15 and 10 million cross only at the seventh validator (combined 140,000,000 SOL, 3,500 bps), so the Nakamoto coefficient is 7, while the largest alone holds just 750 bps. At the exact edge the two tools agree: on a 300-lamport total a single 100-lamport validator is the coefficient on its own - exactly a third offline is enough - and 99 lamports falls one lamport short. A list that never reaches the line reports no coefficient and the exact shortfall. Enter the largest validators in any order (the model ranks them); several validators run by one operator would make the true entity-level figure smaller. Stakes are user-entered. An educational model of the Nakamoto coefficient and finality, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

85. Wrapped SOL planner - what you fund is not what you can spend

Every SOL-side leg of a Solana swap trades wrapped SOL (wSOL) - native SOL held in an ordinary SPL token account for the native mint - because pools and order books only speak SPL tokens. The token program's syncNative instruction sets that account's token balance to its lamport balance minus the rent-exempt reserve, and a token account is 165 bytes, so the reserve is 2,039,280 lamports (Tool 3's figure at that size). Funding a fresh wSOL account with 10 SOL therefore leaves 9,997,960,720 lamports (9.99796072 SOL) of spendable wSOL. The reserve is a deposit, not a fee: close the account and its whole remaining balance comes back, so spending every spendable lamport and then closing still returns the 2,039,280. Splitting multiplies it - 10 SOL across 10 fresh accounts locks 20,392,800 and spends 9,979,607,200 - while topping up an account whose reserve is already funded credits the full transfer. Fund less than the reserve (0.001 SOL into a fresh account is 1,039,280 lamports short) and the spendable balance is zero. An educational model of wrapped SOL and syncNative, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

84. Delinquent stake planner - how much stake can go offline before finality stalls

Tower BFT finalizes a block only when more than two-thirds of the activated stake has voted for it - exactly two-thirds is not a supermajority. Stake whose validator has stopped voting is delinquent, so the largest delinquent stake a cluster can carry is one lamport under a third of the total, never a third itself. On the headline figures - 400,000,000 SOL activated, 25,000,000 SOL delinquent (625 bps, 6.25%) - the cluster can carry 133,333,333.333333333 SOL delinquent, leaving 108,333,333.333333333 SOL of headroom; another 100,000,000 SOL going offline still finalizes, with 8,333,333.333333333 SOL to spare. The edge is exact: at 300 SOL total and 100 SOL delinquent, the online stake is exactly two-thirds, finality stalls - and one lamport of stake returning restores it. While finality is stalled, blocks can still be produced; nothing is settled. The totals are figures you enter - this model reads no live cluster data, and which validators are delinquent, and why, is explorer data. Leader schedule share is Tool 80 and validator commission is Tool 79. An educational model of delinquent stake and finality, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

83. Stake account funding planner — what you fund is not what gets delegated

A stake account is 200 bytes, so its rent-exempt reserve (Tool 3) is 2,282,880 lamports — and that reserve sits inside the account's balance. The stake program never delegates that part: it earns no rewards, and the delegated amount alone — the funding minus the reserve — must clear the cluster's minimum delegation. Fund an account with 10 SOL against an entered minimum of 1 SOL and the delegation is 9,997,717,120 lamports (9.99771712 SOL), not ten; the smallest funding that can delegate at that minimum is 1,002,282,880 lamports, and 1.001 SOL falls 1,282,880 lamports short of it. Splitting multiplies the reserve instead of sharing it: the same 10 SOL opens at most 9 minimum-sized accounts, each paying its own reserve. The minimum itself is a cluster setting — the RPC reports it (getStakeMinimumDelegation) and governance can change it — so you enter it here rather than trusting a printed constant. Activation timing is Tool 24, rewards Tool 8 and commission Tool 79; nothing here reads a live account. An educational model of stake account funding, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

82. Rent reclaim planner — what closing accounts actually gives back

The rent-exempt deposit (Tool 3) is a deposit, not a fee: an account's lamports are locked, not spent, and closing the account returns its entire balance to a destination account. That is why wallets offer to close empty token accounts — every SPL token account is 165 bytes, so each one closed reclaims exactly 2,039,280 lamports, the full (128 + 165) × 3,480 × 2 deposit. Any other account reclaims the same formula at its own size. Closing is not free, though: the closing transactions still pay the base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature (Tool 5), so the net reclaim is the gross reclaim minus the fees of every closing transaction in the plan — batching many closes under few signatures is what makes small accounts worth closing. At the headline settings — closing 10 token accounts and 2 other accounts of 82 bytes (a mint's size, 1,461,600 lamports each) under 1 signature — the token accounts return 20,392,800 lamports, the others 2,923,200, a gross reclaim of 23,316,000 lamports (0.023316 SOL); the signature costs 5,000, so the net reclaim is 23,311,000 lamports (0.023311 SOL). A plan whose signatures cost more than its accounts hold nets negative, and this planner says so rather than hiding it. Two honest limits: an account holding tokens cannot close until its balance is zero — balances are not modelled here — and transfer fees withheld inside a token account are not the holder's to reclaim; they sit in the account until the mint's withdraw authority harvests them. Nothing here reads a live account. An educational model of rent reclaim on account close, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

81. Priority fee percentile planner — which price of the recent sample your bid actually beats

The priority fee is optional and paid per compute unit, on top of the fixed base fee — and Solana's fee market is local: a transaction competes only with transactions writing to the same accounts it touches. Estimators therefore sample the recent prioritization fees paid for the accounts their transaction locks (the RPC's recent-fee cache covers the last 150 slots) and set the compute-unit price to a percentile of that sample: the median for routine work, a high percentile when the account is contested. This planner runs the percentile math on samples you group into four ascending price levels: the percentile is the nearest-rank method — rank = ceil(percentile × N ÷ 100), and the chosen price is the first level whose cumulative count reaches the rank. The fee is the prioritization ceiling (Tool 5), ceil(CU limit × price ÷ 1,000,000), charged on the limit requested, and the sample median is priced too, so the cost of bidding above it is visible in lamports. At the headline settings — samples of 40 transactions at 0, 30 at 1,000, 20 at 10,000 and 10 at 100,000 micro-lamports per compute unit, a 75th percentile, and a 200,000 compute-unit limit — there are 100 samples, rank 75 lands on a price of 10,000 micro-lamports (90 of the 100 samples, 9,000 bps, paid at or below it), the prioritization fee is 2,000 lamports (0.000002 SOL), the sample median price is 1,000 micro-lamports which would pay 200 lamports, so the 75th percentile costs 1,800 lamports more per transaction. At the 40th percentile the chosen price is 0 — a zero fee is still a bid when the account is quiet. A caveat worth knowing: the RPC's own recent-fee values are per-slot minimums — the cheapest fee that landed that slot, a floor rather than a distribution — so the percentile method needs a real sample distribution (a provider's percentile API or your own landed-transaction records), which is why this tool asks for your samples rather than reading any. The samples are yours, the fee market is local to the accounts you touch, and no live fee data is read. An educational model of priority fee percentile selection, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

80. Leader schedule share calculator — how much of an epoch's block production a stake expects

Before an epoch begins, the runtime computes its leader schedule: every slot of the epoch is assigned to a validator in runs of 4 consecutive slots — a leader window — chosen by a stake-weighted random draw, so a validator's expected share of the epoch's windows is exactly its share of the total activated stake. A normal epoch holds 432,000 slots (Tool 12), which is 108,000 windows. At the headline settings — a validator stake of 1,000,000 against a total of 500,000,000 — the share is 20 bps (0.2%), the expectation is 216 windows, 864 slots, and at an assumed skipped share of 250 bps, 21.6 of those slots are expected to be skipped and 842.4 to produce blocks. One expected window corresponds to 4,629.629629629 of total activated stake. The expectation is a mean, not the schedule: the actual draw is seeded, fixed for the epoch and knowable in advance, and a real count lands near the expectation rather than on it. A stake below one basis point of the total still has a non-zero expectation — a fraction of a window, meaning an assignment in some epochs and none in others. The slots input accepts any multiple of 4 up to 432,000, so a warmup epoch can be modelled too. The skip share is your own assumed figure — not a live delinquency rate — and no live stake or schedule data is read. Commission and rewards are Tool 79 and Tool 8, not this model. An educational model of the leader schedule, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

79. Validator commission calculator — the cut taken from staking rewards before you see them

Delegate SOL to a validator and the rewards your stake earns are split once, at the source: the validator's vote account keeps a commission share of the rewards, and the rest is distributed to the delegated stake accounts pro-rata by active stake weight, as immediately active stake. Commission is set in whole percentage points — an integer from 0 to 100, never basis points; it can be changed only during the first half of an epoch; and a vote account created without setting one defaults to 100%, which passes nothing to delegators at all. The charge falls on rewards only, never on principal — even a 100% commission leaves the delegated stake itself untouched. What survives is arithmetic: at the headline settings — a stake of 100, an assumed gross reward rate of 700 bps (7%) a year and a commission of 5% — the gross reward is 7 a year, the validator keeps 0.35, and the delegator nets 6.65, an effective rate of 665 bps. Each single percentage point of commission is worth 0.07 a year at these settings. Over 30 days the gross reward is 0.575342465, the commission takes 0.028767123 and the delegator nets 0.546575342. At a 0% commission the effective rate equals the gross rate exactly; at 10% the same stake would net 6.3 a year. The gross rate is your own assumed figure for one validator — no live rate or validator is read. This is direct delegation only: a stake pool's token fees are Tool 75, gross epoch compounding before commission is Tool 8, and a validator's separate block-revenue or MEV commission is not modelled. An educational model of validator commission, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

78. Transaction fee split calculator — where a transaction's fees actually go

Tool 5 prices what a transaction pays; this tool follows the money. Every transaction pays a base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature, split exactly in half: 2,500 lamports burned — destroyed, out of the supply — and 2,500 to the block leader who included the transaction. The prioritization fee (Tool 5's own figure: the ceiling of compute-unit limit × compute-unit price ÷ 1,000,000, charged on the limit requested) is not split at all: since SIMD-0096 activated in February 2025, 100% of it goes to the leader — before that change, half of it burned too. So the burned share of a total fee falls as the priority bid rises, while the burn itself never moves: with no prioritization fee exactly 5,000 bps (50%) of what is paid burns. At the headline settings — 1 signature, a 200,000 compute-unit limit at 100,000 micro-lamports — the prioritization fee is 20,000 lamports of a 25,000-lamport total, so only 2,500 burns, a burned share of 1,000 bps (10%), and 22,500 reaches the leader. Across 10,000 such transactions the fees total 250,000,000 lamports, of which 25,000,000 burn (0.025 SOL) and 225,000,000 go to leaders. A transaction that lands but fails still pays its fee in full, split the same way. The v1 format's absolute-fee form and proposed base-fee redesigns are not modelled; Jito tips are a separate transfer (Tool 77), and rent is a locked deposit, not a fee (Tool 3). An educational model of transaction fee splits, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

77. Bundle tip planner — what landing a Jito bundle actually costs

A bundle is a group of at most 5 transactions sent to the Jito Block Engine, executed sequentially and atomically — all of them land in the same slot or none do. Inclusion is an auction: the bundle carries a tip, a plain SOL transfer to one of the 8 Jito tip accounts inside one of its transactions, and the minimum tip is 1,000 lamports (the competitive floor moves with demand and is not modelled here). Every transaction still pays its base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature (Tool 5) on top. At the headline settings — a tip of 1,000,000 lamports (0.001 SOL), 3 transactions at 1 signature each, and an expected gross profit of 2,500,000 lamports — the base fees are 15,000 lamports, one landed bundle costs 1,015,000 and keeps 1,485,000: a cost share of 4,060 bps (40.6%) of the profit, taken by the auction. The most that could be tipped before the base fees alone eat the profit is 2,485,000 lamports — the break-even tip — and across 100 landed bundles the all-in cost is 101,500,000 lamports (0.1015 SOL). A bundle that does not land executes nothing and pays nothing: no tip, no base fees. This is the bundle route; Tool 14 models what a loose slippage tolerance lets a sandwicher take through the ordinary route. Priority fees, the live tip floor, landing probability and retries are not modelled, and no live bundle is read. An educational model of bundle tips, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

76. Farm emissions calculator — what a liquidity-mining program actually pays you

A farm — the incentive program a pool runs on Raydium, Orca or any Solana venue — pays a reward token at a stated emission per day, split pro-rata by staked value: your share is your stake ÷ the total staked, and that share of each day's emission is yours. At the headline settings — an emission of 10,000 reward tokens a day at a price of 0.5, a total of 2,000,000 staked and your stake of 10,000 (a 0.5% share, 50 bps) — you earn 50 tokens a day, 1,500 over 30 days, worth 25 a day and 750 over the period: a simple APR of 91.25%. The lesson is in the APR's formula — emission × price × 365 ÷ total staked — because your stake cancels out of it: a position twice as large earns twice the tokens at exactly the same APR, and if the total staked doubles, the APR halves to 45.625% with no rate ever changing — a fixed emission pie is diluted by every new staker. Compounding the daily rate for a year gives 148.7707%, but only under the stated assumption that rewards are reinvested daily at the same rate and price. This is not a pool's trading fees (Tool 71) and not protocol staking (Tool 8). Emission schedules end, reward tokens can be locked, and no live farm is read. An educational model of farm emissions, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

75. Stake pool fee model — the three fees an LST charges, each on a different base

Every big Solana liquid staking token runs on the SPL stake pool program (Tool 15), and the program charges its fees in pool tokens, on three different bases. Going in, the SOL deposit fee is deducted from the tokens your deposit mints. Each epoch, the epoch fee takes a share of that epoch’s rewards only — paid by minting new pool tokens to the manager, so no balance moves and every holder is diluted instead. Coming out, the SOL withdrawal fee is deducted from your tokens before the rest convert back to SOL. At the headline settings — a pool holding 1,000,000 for 800,000 pool tokens (a rate of 1.25), a deposit of 10,000, a 100 bps deposit fee, one epoch earning 8,000 of rewards at a 1,000 bps epoch fee, and a 50 bps withdrawal fee — the deposit mints 8,000 tokens and the deposit fee deducts 80, leaving 7,920; the epoch fee is 800 of value, minted as 640 new tokens; and withdrawing pays 9,920.665809259 — a net of −79.334190741 — against 10,079.207920792 for the identical round trip with no fees at all, a drag of 158.542111533. The deposit and withdrawal fees tax the principal, so a one-epoch stay can lose money in a pool that earned rewards. Referral splits, stake (non-SOL) deposits and validator changes are not modelled. An educational model of stake pool fees, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

74. Transfer-fee swap model — what a fee-on-transfer token does to a swap, twice

A Token-2022 mint can carry a transfer fee (Tool 13): every transfer withholds ceil(amount × bps ÷ 10,000), capped at the mint’s maximum fee, in raw units. When such a token trades on a constant-product pool, that tax lands twice around the swap: the pool is credited only what survives the input transfer, it swaps that smaller amount, and the output transfer withholds again before your wallet is credited — so a quote that ignores the fees overstates what lands. At the headline settings — reserves of 1,000,000,000 raw units on each side, sending 10,000,000, a 25 bps pool fee, a 100 bps input transfer fee and a 50 bps output transfer fee — the input transfer withholds 100,000, the pool swaps the 9,900,000 it was credited and sends 9,778,683 out, and the output transfer withholds 48,894 more, so your wallet is credited 9,729,789 — a shortfall of 146,693 against the 9,876,482 the same trade would return with no transfer fees at all. A maximum-fee cap can bind on either side and shrink that side’s tax; a 100% output fee honestly credits 0. All amounts are raw units throughout — convert with your mint’s decimals (Tool 6). The pool is modelled as pricing the amount it is credited; a real venue’s handling of withheld fees can differ in detail. An educational model of a transfer-fee swap, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

73. Leveraged loop calculator — recursive supply and borrow (“multiply”)

A leverage loop — the mechanic behind “multiply” products on Solana lending markets — deposits an asset, borrows against it at the market’s maximum LTV, redeposits what it borrowed, borrows against that, and repeats. Each round borrows the same fraction of the last, so the rounds form a geometric series with a closed-form limit: total supplied = deposit ÷ (1 − LTV), total borrowed = supplied − deposit, and effective leverage = supplied ÷ deposit. At the headline LTV of 7,500 bps (75%), a deposit of 1,000 loops to 4,000 supplied against 3,000 borrowed — 4x leverage — and the loop multiplies the spread, not just the yield: at a 500 bps supply rate against a 700 bps borrow rate it earns 200 and pays 210 over a year, a net of −10, an APY of −1% on the deposit, breaking even only at a supply rate of 525 bps (the borrow rate times the LTV). The fully looped position’s health factor is the liquidation threshold divided by the LTV — 1.066666666 at an 8,000 bps threshold — because the supplied side cancels: loop further and the buffer thins, even with the same asset on both sides. Both rates and the LTV are frozen for the year; price moves, rate moves, per-round transaction costs and venue leverage caps are not modelled. An educational model of a leveraged loop, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

Solana in 30 seconds

Accounts & lamports

Everything on Solana is an account: wallets, tokens, programs and data all live in accounts holding lamports, the smallest unit of SOL (1 SOL = 1,000,000,000 lamports). Balances are whole lamports — a SOL amount never has more than 9 decimal places.

Programs

Smart contracts are called programs. They are stateless code; the state they work on lives in separate accounts they own. The SPL Token program, for example, manages every classic Solana token through token accounts it owns.

Fees & rent

A transaction pays a base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature, plus optional priority fees. Accounts pay rent for the data they store — or hold two years of rent up front (the rent-exempt minimum) and pay none again.

Speed

Proof of History gives the network a shared clock before consensus, so validators can order transactions fast: blocks land roughly every 400 milliseconds and finality follows in seconds. That speed is why Solana hosts busy DEXs, payment rails and consumer apps.

Tools — educational models

Each tool runs entirely in your browser and is labelled for what it is: a model that teaches the mechanic, never a live quote.

1. SOL ⇄ lamports converter

Exact conversion with integer math — no rounding, no floating point. Fill in one side and convert.

2. Constant-product swap model

The x·y=k mechanic behind AMM pools across Solana (Raydium CP-Swap, Orca and others): the fee comes off your input, the rest trades against the reserves, and the price moves against you as size grows.

3. Rent-exempt minimum calculator

What an account must hold to be rent-exempt: (128 + data length) × 3,480 lamports per byte-year × 2 years. Try 0 for a plain wallet account, 82 for a token mint, 165 for a token account.

4. Impermanent-loss calculator

For a 50/50 constant-product position: how far the LP position trails simply holding, when the price moves by the ratio you enter — the cost that pool fees have to beat.

5. Transaction fee estimator — base + priority fee

What a transaction costs before rent: a base fee of 5,000 lamports per signature, plus an optional priority fee — ceil(compute-unit limit × compute-unit price ÷ 1,000,000) lamports, where the price is in micro-lamports per compute unit. The priority fee is charged on the limit you request, not the compute you use, and a transaction's limit is capped at 1,400,000 compute units.

6. SPL token amount converter — UI amount ⇄ raw units

SOL's 9 decimals (Tool 1) are just one mint's choice. Every SPL mint sets its own decimals — USDC uses 6, many mints use 9, some use 0 — and token accounts store raw integer amounts in the mint's smallest unit. Wallets show the UI amount: raw ÷ 10^decimals. Fill in one side, with the mint's decimals.

7. Solana address checker — base58 format

Every Solana address is 32 bytes written in base58, the alphabet that omits 0, O, I and l so addresses can't be misread. Paste an address and check its format locally, before you trust it anywhere: every character must be base58 and the decoded value must be exactly 32 bytes. A pasted address with stray extra characters decodes to the wrong length and fails here. This checks format only — not that the account exists, holds anything, or is the address you meant.

8. Staking rewards estimator — epoch compounding

Staking pays once per epoch — 432,000 slots, about 2 days at the target slot time, so about 182.5 epochs a year. Rewards land in the stake account and compound from the next epoch; validators take their commission as a percentage of the reward, not of your stake. Enter a stake, an annual yield you assume, a validator commission, and a number of epochs. The yield is your assumption — this is not a live network yield.

9. Solana Pay URL builder & inspector — transfer requests

Solana Pay encodes a payment request as one URL: solana:<recipient>?amount=…&spl-token=…&reference=…&label=…&message=…&memo=… — the string inside payment QR codes across the ecosystem. The recipient is the base58 address of a native SOL account: associated token accounts must not be used as the recipient, because for a token payment the wallet derives the recipient's token account from the mint you name in spl-token. The amount is in user units — SOL, not lamports; a token's UI amount, not raw units — 0 is valid, leaving it out means the wallet asks the payer, and an amount finer than the asset's decimals (9 for SOL, the mint's own for a token) is malformed. Label and message are display-only; a memo is written on-chain in an SPL Memo instruction and is public forever, so never put anything private in it. A reference is an extra read-only key the wallet attaches to the transfer, so the payment can be found by it later. 11. Slippage tolerance & minimum-received calculator

Every DEX interface on Solana — Jupiter, Raydium, Orca — asks for a slippage tolerance before it sends your swap. The tolerance is the most the price may move against you between the quote and the transaction landing: the wallet computes a minimum received (expected out × (1 − tolerance)) for an exact-in swap, or a maximum input (expected in × (1 + tolerance)) for an exact-out swap, and the on-chain program aborts the swap instead of filling past that bound. Both bounds are rounded against you on purpose — minimum received floors, maximum input floors — so the bound you sign is never looser than the tolerance you picked: an expected 4 at 0.5% protects at 3.98, never rounded back up to 4. Too tight a tolerance and ordinary price movement (or another trade landing first) fails your swap and costs the fee anyway; too loose and a worse fill — including a sandwich attack — still passes. Enter an expected output, an expected input, and a tolerance in basis points (25 bps = 0.25%, 50 bps = 0.5%, 100 bps = 1%). An educational model of the bound, not a live quote.

12. Epoch schedule converter — slot ⇄ epoch, warmup included

Almost every Solana explainer says an epoch is 432,000 slots — and then code quietly computes epoch = slot ÷ 432,000. That formula is wrong, and it has been wrong since genesis: the chain warms up. Epoch 0 holds 32 slots and each warmup epoch doubles (32, 64, 128, …), so the first normal epoch is epoch 14, starting at slot 524,256 — at that slot the naive division says "epoch 1". Only from there is an epoch a constant 432,000 slots, and the correct formula carries the offset: epoch = 14 + (slot − 524,256) ÷ 432,000. Epoch boundaries are when staking rewards land, stake activates and cools down, and leader schedules rotate, so an epoch computed the naive way mis-times all of them. Convert a slot to its epoch, or an epoch to its slot range — both run locally. This is schedule math in slots only: it deliberately does not convert to dates or times, because the target slot duration has changed repeatedly (400 ms for years, cut in stages during 2026) and skipped slots stretch real time further; for a slot's actual timestamp, use getBlockTime or an explorer. An educational model of the runtime's EpochSchedule, not live chain data.

13. Token-2022 transfer fee calculator — withheld on every transfer

Some Solana tokens are minted under the Token-2022 program with a transfer-fee extension: on every transfer the sender is debited the full amount, the recipient is credited the amount minus a fee, and the fee stays withheld inside the recipient's token account until it is harvested to the mint and withdrawn. The classic SPL Token program has no transfer fees — the same transfer of a classic token delivers in full. The program computes the fee on raw amounts (the mint's smallest units — convert with Tool 6): fee = ceil(amount × basis points ÷ 10,000), capped at the mint's maximum fee, and 0 when the rate or the amount is 0. The ceiling matters: any positive transfer at any positive rate pays at least 1 raw unit, so dust transfers of a fee-bearing token can deliver nothing at all. A mint stores an older and a newer fee and picks by epoch; this model prices the single configuration you enter. Work a transfer's fee, or gross up — how much to send so the recipient receives an exact amount. An educational model of the program's fee math, not a live quote.

14. Sandwich attack (MEV) model — what a loose tolerance lets through

A sandwich attack is the canonical MEV extraction on an AMM: an attacker who can see a pending swap buys the same direction first (the front-run pushes the pool price against the victim), lets the victim fill at the worse price, then sells everything back into the price the victim's own trade moved (the back-run). This model runs all three legs through the hub's own constant-product swap (Tool 2) against the reserves each leg leaves behind. The victim's defence is the slippage bound (Tool 11): if the sandwiched fill falls below the minimum received at the victim's tolerance, the victim's transaction aborts — the sandwich makes nothing and the attacker loses the round-trip costs unwinding alone. At a loose tolerance the fill passes its own protection and the attacker's profit comes straight out of the victim's worse price; it is extracted, not created. Defences in the real ecosystem include tight tolerances and private transaction routing that keeps a swap out of the public flow. An educational model of a sandwich on a single pool, not live MEV data.

15. Liquid staking (LST) exchange rate — why the rate rises, not your balance

A liquid staking pool (the SPL Stake Pool program, Marinade, Jito and their peers) holds staked SOL and issues a pool token. That token does not rebase: when staking rewards land, your token balance stays exactly the same — instead the pool's total SOL grows with no new tokens minted, so the exchange rate (total staked SOL ÷ token supply, in SOL per token) rises. Depositing mints deposit × supply ÷ total tokens and redeeming returns redeem × total ÷ supply SOL, both floored like an on-chain division — a dust deposit can floor to zero tokens. This model adds a rewards amount you supply to the total so you can watch the same deposit mint fewer tokens afterwards and the same redemption return more SOL: that difference is the yield. It charges no deposit, withdrawal or epoch fees (real pools charge some — check the pool's own terms) and the unstake delay itself is priced by Tool 109's exit planner: a withdrawal not covered by pool liquidity deactivates stake and waits out the epoch cooldown. An educational model of one pool's exchange rate, not a live pool's rate. Not financial advice.

16. Program-derived address (PDA) finder — seeds, bump and the curve check

Programs own accounts and "sign" without any private key through program-derived addresses: SHA-256(seeds ‖ bump ‖ program ID ‖ "ProgramDerivedAddress"), kept only when the 32-byte hash is not a valid ed25519 point — an on-curve result could have a private key, so it can never be a PDA. 17. CLMM position amounts — what a liquidity amount holds

A concentrated-liquidity position on Raydium CLMM or Orca Whirlpools is not opened by handing over a chosen amount of each token. You pick a liquidity amount L and a tick range (Tool 10), and the token amounts follow from the square-root prices: inside the range the position holds L × (1/√Pcurrent − 1/√Pupper) of token0 and L × (√Pcurrent − √Plower) of token1; at or below the lower tick it holds only token0, at or above the upper tick only token1 — the composition slides continuously from one asset to the other as the price crosses the range, which is exactly the position's price exposure. 18. LP deposit & pool share — what a deposit mints

Adding liquidity to a constant-product pool (Tool 2) on Raydium CP, Orca or any x·y=k AMM mints LP tokens in proportion to the smaller side's contribution: minted = supply × min(depositA ÷ reserveA, depositB ÷ reserveB), floored in exact integer math. Only the part of the deposit that matches the pool's ratio is used — the excess of the other token buys no extra LP tokens, so a good interface sizes the deposit to the ratio or returns the excess. The first deposit into an empty pool is different: it sets the starting price and mints the geometric mean, sqrt(depositA × depositB). Headline: against reserves of 1,000,000 A / 4,000,000 B with 2,000,000 LP tokens outstanding, depositing 10,000 A / 50,000 B mints 20,000 LP tokens (0.990099% of the pool): token A limits the mint, only 40,000 of the B is used, and 10,000 B is excess. Amounts are in the tokens' own units — convert with each mint's decimals (Tool 6). An educational model, not a live pool quote. Not financial advice.

19. Token vesting calculator - cliff & linear unlock

Team, investor and ecosystem allocations on Solana commonly vest on a cliff + linear schedule (the shape Jupiter Lock, Streamflow and most launch allocations use): the allocation accrues linearly over the full duration from day one - accrued = total x elapsed / duration, floored at the token's smallest unit - but nothing is claimable before the cliff, and at the cliff the whole accrued share releases at once. 20. Lending health & liquidation calculator

Solana lending markets (Kamino, Marginfi, Drift and peers) judge a borrow position by its health factor: health = (collateral value x liquidation threshold) / debt, where the collateral value is amount x price and the loan-to-value is debt / value. Below a health factor of 1 a market may liquidate - the price where health equals exactly 1 is the liquidation price, debt / (amount x threshold). Borrowing power is set by the lower max LTV, not the threshold: the gap between the two percentages is the market's safety buffer. Headline: 10 tokens at a price of 100 against 500 of debt at an 80% liquidation threshold and a 75% max LTV is worth 1,000 - an LTV of 50%, a health factor of 1.6, a max borrow total of 750 (250 still available), and a liquidation price of 62.5, a fall of about 37.5%. Single collateral and single debt, no interest accrual and no liquidation bonus or fee modelled - real markets weight several assets and accrue interest continuously, so a real health factor drifts even at a flat price. An educational model, not a live lending quote. Not financial advice.

21. Transaction size & packet-limit calculator

Every Solana transaction must fit a single network packet: 1,232 bytes - and its serialized size is exact wire math, not an estimate. Signatures cost a shortvec count plus 64 bytes each, the message header 3 bytes, each static account key a shortvec count plus 32 bytes, the recent blockhash 32 bytes, and one instruction a count byte, a 1-byte program index, a shortvec count plus 1 byte per referenced account, and a shortvec length plus its data. A shortvec length costs 1 byte below 128, 2 below 16,384. Headline: a plain SOL transfer - 1 signature, 3 static keys, 2 instruction accounts, 12 data bytes - is exactly 215 bytes. In a v0 transaction, accounts loaded through an address lookup table cost a 1-byte index each instead of 32 bytes - but the table reference itself costs 32 bytes plus two shortvec counts, so loading a single account through a table makes the transaction 4 bytes bigger; tables pay off from a handful of accounts up (the same transfer with its two non-payer keys in one table is 188 bytes, saving 27). One instruction modelled - each extra instruction adds its own program-index byte, two shortvec counts, account indices and data - and exactly one lookup table; durable-nonce transactions are not modelled. An educational model of the wire format, not a live transaction. Not financial advice.

22. Anchor account space planner

Every account an Anchor program creates starts with an 8-byte discriminator, then its fields serialized by Borsh at fixed sizes — no padding, no alignment: bool and u8 take 1 byte, u16 takes 2, u32 takes 4, u64 takes 8, u128 takes 16 and a Pubkey takes 32. Variable types are sized by the maximum the program declares: a String or a byte vector costs a 4-byte length prefix plus its capacity, a Vec costs 4 plus count × element size, an array costs count × element size, and an Option costs 1 tag byte plus its inner type. Headline: a vault struct of Pubkey + u64 + bool + a 32-character String is 8 + 32 + 8 + 1 + 36 = 85 bytes, locking 1,482,480 lamports as its rent-exempt deposit — space is the deposit, so over-allocating wastes SOL permanently and under-allocating fails the first write that outgrows it. Four fields modelled (structs with more add linearly); enums are not modelled — add 1 byte plus the largest variant per enum by hand. An educational model of the serialization format, not a live account. Not financial advice.

23. Sale split calculator — platform fee, creator royalty, seller remainder

Every marketplace sale and escrow release on Solana splits one price three ways: the platform takes a fee in basis points, the creator takes a royalty in basis points, and the seller receives what is left. The two cuts floor at the smallest unit, like on-chain integer math, and the seller is the remainder — price minus the two floored cuts, never a third independently rounded percentage — so the three parts always sum to the price exactly and no unit is created or destroyed by rounding. The seller keeps the flooring dust too: a 3-unit price split 50/50 pays 1 unit to each cut and 1 unit to the seller. Headline: a sale at 100 with a 200 bps platform fee and a 500 bps royalty pays 2 to the platform, 5 to the creator and 93 to the seller. Both cuts come out of the price in this model — a venue that charges the buyer a fee on top splits differently — and creator royalties on classic Solana NFTs are a marketplace convention (programmable NFTs can enforce them on-chain). An educational model of a sale split, not a live marketplace quote. Not financial advice.

24. Stake activation & cooldown timer — the 9% warmup/cooldown queue

Delegated stake never switches on or off at once. At each epoch boundary the stake program lets only a rate-limited share of the cluster change state: an account's allowed change is its portion ÷ the total moving the same way × the cluster's previous-epoch effective stake × the warmup/cooldown rate, floored, never more than its own remaining portion, and always at least 1 lamport while a portion remains. The rate is 900 bps (9%) on the current network — it was 2,500 bps (25%) before the reduce_stake_warmup_cooldown feature — so at most 9% of the cluster's effective stake may activate or deactivate per epoch, shared pro-rata by everything moving that epoch. That is why a normal stake (far below 9% of the cluster) is fully effective after one epoch, while a whale-sized movement, or a crowd all heading for the exit together, queues over several. Headline: 40,000,000 SOL activating solo against a 400,000,000 SOL cluster moves 36,000,000 in epoch 1 and the last 4,000,000 in epoch 2. This model holds the cluster's effective stake constant at your figure (on-chain it is last epoch's stake-history entry and drifts) and assumes other movers drain at the same fractional pace. An educational model of stake warmup and cooldown, not a live staking quote. Not financial advice.

25. CLMM uncollected fees calculator — fee growth since the last touch

A concentrated-liquidity position (Raydium CLMM, Orca Whirlpools) never stores a running "fees earned" total. The pool keeps fee-growth counters: fees accrued per unit of liquidity, in X64 fixed point (scaled by 264) and stored as u128 integers — a global counter per token, an "outside" counter per tick, and in each position the fee growth inside its range at its last touch. The growth inside is global − below the lower tick − above the upper tick (the SDK computes it for you), and what the position has earned since that touch is floor((inside now − inside last) × liquidity ÷ 264), plus whatever an earlier touch already settled into its tokens-owed balance. The subtraction wraps modulo 2128, exactly like the on-chain u128 arithmetic: a counter that has passed its stored value on the way around is not an error, and its delta is still the true small increment. That is also why the counters are integers far beyond float range — this calculator takes them as whole integers and does exact BigInt math, never a float. Headline: liquidity 1,000,000 with token A's growth up exactly 264 (1.0 per unit of liquidity) accrues exactly 1,000,000 raw units. The growth counters are inputs here, as an explorer or the SDK reports them — this tool reads no live position. An educational model of CLMM fee accounting, not a live position quote. Not financial advice.

26. Inflation schedule calculator — how fast new SOL is issued

SOL has no supply cap: new SOL is issued every epoch as staking rewards, at a rate set by the network's inflation schedule — three parameters in the validator code: an initial rate of 8% per year, a disinflation rate of 15% per year, and a long-term terminal rate of 1.5% the schedule never falls below: rate = max(1.5%, 8% × (1 − disinflation)years). The year is fractional on-chain — the validator recomputes the rate every epoch from the fraction of a year elapsed since inflation began at epoch 150 (February 2021), so the rate glides down continuously rather than stepping once a year. Headline: at year 5 the rate is 3.5496%, which on a supply of 500,000,000 SOL issues 17,748,212.5 new SOL that year; the terminal floor arrives at year ≈10.30 (the first whole year at 1.5% is year 11). The disinflation rate is an input so the governance change adopted in 2026 — doubling it to 30%, which would pull the floor forward to year ≈4.69 — can be modelled honestly: it is not yet implemented in the validator, so 1,500 bps (15%) remains the shipped schedule. 30. Token valuation calculator — market cap & fully diluted valuation (FDV)

36. Perpetual position margin, PnL & liquidation price

A perpetual position is judged on its margin, not its size: at leverage L you post initial margin = notional ÷ leverage, your unrealised PnL is (mark − entry) × size for a long (mirrored for a short), and your equity is margin + PnL. The venue liquidates when equity falls to the maintenance margin — a rate (an input here: every venue sets its own, often tiered by size) times the current notional — which is why liquidation strikes while money still remains: the headline long of 100 units at 150, 10x leverage, a 500 bps maintenance rate posts 1,500 of margin and is liquidated at 142.105263157, a fall of only about 5.26%, even though its margin would not reach zero until 135. A long at leverage 1 or below has no liquidation price at all — its margin covers the whole notional, so no fall can reach the line. 37. Stableswap swap model — the invariant that prices stable pools near 1:1

Pools of like-valued tokens (a USDC/USDT pair) do not use the constant-product curve of Tool 2 — that curve prices every pool as if its tokens could diverge, and charges for it. A stableswap pool (the Curve-style invariant behind Saber and other stable pools on Solana) blends a constant-sum line with constant-product: its invariant D is solved from the balances and an amplification coefficient A — as A grows the price holds near 1:1 while the pool stays balanced, and as the pool unbalances (or A falls) the curve bends back toward constant-product pricing. The headline pool of 1,000,000 / 1,000,000 at A = 100 has D = 2,000,000 and returns a 10,000 trade at 9,999.502463058 gross — a price impact of under 0.005%, against 9,900 on the constant-product curve — before its 4 bps fee, charged on the output, leaves 9,995.502662073. Unbalance the same pool to 1,500,000 / 500,000 and the trade returns 9,910.453997586 gross: the curve defends the peg only while liquidity backs it. 40. Exact-out swap model - what receiving an exact amount costs

Tool 2 prices a swap by what you put in; an exact-out order fixes the other end - the payment, invoice or arbitrage leg that must receive an exact amount - and asks what it costs to get it. 41. Arbitrage profit model - what aligning a pool to an outside price earns

A pool never learns the price anywhere else by itself - arbitrageurs teach it, by buying whichever token the pool prices too cheaply until the prices meet. 42. CLMM swap model - a swap inside one tick range

Tools 10, 17 and 25 price a concentrated-liquidity position; this one trades against it. 43. Limit-order book walk - what a market buy actually pays

Every other swap tool on this hub prices against a pool curve. A central limit order book (Phoenix, OpenBook and Manifest on Solana) works the other way: traders rest limit orders at their own prices, and a market buy walks the ask side - it fills at the best (lowest) ask first, then the next, at each resting order's own price, until the buy is filled or the book runs out. The headline book - 50 at 100, 100 at 101, 200 at 103 - fills a buy of 120 as 50 at 100 plus 70 at 101, for a total cost of 12,070 and a volume-weighted average price (VWAP) of 100.583333333: exactly 70 more than the 12,000 the same fill would cost if the whole book sat at the best ask. Each level's cost floors at the smallest unit in exact scaled-BigInt. Three ask levels are modelled; a live book has many more, orders ahead of yours in time priority, and venue taker fees, which are stated here as not modelled. An educational model of an order-book fill, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

44. TWAP oracle calculator - the time-weighted average price

A lending market or a cautious swap program rarely trusts a pool's spot price - one trade can move it. What it reads instead is the pool's TWAP oracle: the pool adds its current tick to a running tick cumulative counter every second, and the time-weighted average price over any window is 1.0001^(cumulative change ÷ seconds). The headline window - a cumulative that moves from 0 to 360,000 over 3,600 seconds - averages tick 100, a TWAP of 1.010049662, sitting 97.55 bps below a spot price of 1.02: that gap is the lag, the price the pool has been at rather than the price it is at now. The division truncates toward zero, as the on-chain integer math does - a falling market averaging -33 ticks from a -100 change over 3 seconds, not -34 - and the discarded remainder is reported, not hidden. A longer window smooths a spike further and lags a real move longer; that trade-off is the whole design. The cumulatives and the spot are your inputs, as a pool's oracle account or an explorer reports them. An educational model of a TWAP oracle, not a live quote. Not financial advice.

45. LP withdrawal calculator - what burning LP tokens returns

47. Dutch auction price calculator — what a decaying sale price is right now

A Dutch auction sells to the first buyer who accepts the current price: the seller publishes a start price, a floor and a duration, and the price falls between them — linearly in the classic form modelled here. Headline: a sale from 10 down to a floor of 1 over 3,600 seconds stands at 7.75 after 900 seconds (a quarter of the drop gone), 5.5 halfway, and exactly 1 the moment the duration ends — the ends are exact, never asymptotic. 48. Governance quorum & approval calculator — did the DAO vote actually pass?

A token-governance proposal (Realms / SPL Governance and most Solana DAO frameworks) must clear two independent bars, and reading only the for/against split misses the one that kills most proposals. Quorum: enough of the governing supply must participate at all — votes cast (for + against + abstain) must reach the quorum share of supply, ceilinged at the smallest unit. Approval: of the decisive votes, the for-share — for ÷ (for + against), floored to a whole basis point — must reach the threshold. Abstentions count toward quorum but toward neither side of approval — the convention modelled here. Headline: a supply of 10,000,000 with 600,000 for, 200,000 against and 100,000 abstaining, a 10% quorum and a 60% approval threshold — the for-share is a comfortable 75%, yet the proposal fails, because 900,000 cast is 100,000 short of the 1,000,000 quorum. Raise the for-votes to 700,000 and the same tally passes: cast reaches exactly 1,000,000 and the for-share is 77.77%. Votes cast above the supply are rejected — a one-token-one-vote tally cannot cast more than exists. An educational model of a governance vote, not a live tally. Not financial advice.

49. Token supply allocation planner — splitting a fixed supply into buckets

Every token launch fixes its total supply at mint time and splits it into buckets — liquidity, community, team, treasury — stated as shares of the supply. This planner takes the three named shares in basis points and gives the rest to the treasury, the designated remainder bucket. The named buckets are floored at the smallest unit, and four floored shares can sum to less than the supply, so the treasury receives the supply minus the three floored amounts: the parts then sum to the supply exactly, and the treasury absorbs the flooring dust — provably never more than 3 smallest units, and zero at whole-token supplies. 50. Quadratic voting cost calculator — voice priced as the square of votes

Quadratic voting — the mechanism behind DAO grant rounds and governance experiments — prices voice, not votes: casting v votes on one proposal costs v² voice credits from a fixed budget, so each additional vote costs more than the last (the marginal cost of the next vote is 2v + 1: the first costs 1, the tenth costs 19 more). Intensity is expensive and breadth is cheap. Headline: on a budget of 100 credits, casting 6 votes on one proposal and 8 on another costs 36 + 64 = 100 exactly — 14 votes in total — while concentrating the whole budget on a single proposal buys at most 10 votes. Three proposals and whole votes are modelled; each framework sets its own budget, proposal count and credit rules. An educational model of a quadratic vote, not a live ballot. Not financial advice.

51. Token burn & holder-share calculator — a bigger share of a smaller supply

52. Merkle airdrop proof planner — one root on-chain, a proof per claim

An airdrop or allowlist does not store every recipient on-chain: each recipient is a leaf in a Merkle tree, pairs of hashes combine upward to a single root, and only that 32-byte root goes on-chain. A claim then supplies a proof — one sibling hash for each level of the tree — and the program hashes the claim up the proof and checks that it lands on the stored root. The tree is completed to the next power of two: 10,000 recipients need a tree of depth 14 padded to 16,384 leaves (32,767 nodes in total), and every claim carries a proof of 14 hashes, 448 bytes. Proof size moves in steps, not smoothly: 16,384 recipients still fit depth 14 exactly, while recipient 16,385 pushes the whole tree to depth 15 and every proof to 480 bytes. A single recipient is the degenerate case — the leaf is the root and the proof is empty. This model counts the tree and the proof; the leaf contents, the hash function and any claim program are not modelled, and a compressed-NFT tree (Tool 28), which stores its tree on-chain, is a different structure. An educational model of a Merkle proof, not a live airdrop. Not financial advice.

53. Multisig approval planner — how many keys must agree, and how few can block

A multisig wallet (Squads is the common Solana form) holds funds under N members and acts only when M of them approve — every member's vote equal, unlike a token-weighted DAO vote (Tool 48). Three counts describe the setup. A 3-of-5 threshold is 60% of the members, and 16 of the 32 possible member subsets are large enough to approve (10 sets of three, 5 of four, 1 of five). The mirror count is the smallest blocking coalition: N − M + 1 members refusing makes approval unreachable, so a 3-of-5 is blocked by any 3 refusers, a unanimous 5-of-5 hands every single member a veto, and a 1-of-3 can only be blocked if all 3 refuse. Raising M buys safety against stolen keys and sells liveness — more approvals to collect before anything can move. This model counts subsets and coalitions in exact integer math; member identities, key custody, proposal expiry and any live multisig are not modelled. An educational model of a multisig threshold, not a live wallet. Not financial advice.

55. Escrow release & refund planner — the fee is charged only if the trade completes

An escrow trade locks the buyer's payment in a program-controlled account until the seller delivers, so neither side has to trust the other with the money first. 56. DCA execution planner — what splitting one buy into many orders does, and does not, do

A dollar-cost-averaging program (Jupiter's DCA is the common Solana form) splits one intended buy into N equal orders executed over time. 57. DLMM bin price planner — the ladder a discrete-bin pool prices on

A discrete liquidity market maker (Meteora's DLMM is the common Solana form) does not slide along a curve: it splits the price range into bins, each holding one fixed price, and the price at bin id is (1 + binStep ÷ 10000) ^ id — adjacent bins differ by exactly the bin step, so the ladder is purely geometric. 61. Options payoff calculator — what a long call or put is worth at expiry

Beside the perpetuals of Tools 34 and 36, Solana derivatives venues (Drift, Zeta) trade options: a long call pays max(expiry price − strike, 0) per unit at expiry, a long put pays max(strike − expiry price, 0), and the buyer paid the premium up front — so the net is payoff minus premium and the most a long option can ever lose is exactly that premium. The headline long call, strike 150, premium 5, over 100 units, at an expiry price of 180 pays 3,000 against a 500 premium — a net of +2,500, +500% on the premium — and breaks even at exactly 155, the strike plus the premium (a put breaks even at strike minus premium: the mirror put at an expiry of 120 nets the same +2,500 and breaks even at 145). 62. Blockhash expiry checker — how long a signed transaction can still land

Every Solana transaction carries a recent blockhash, and the runtime only processes it while that blockhash is recent enough: the maximum processing age is 150 blocks, which is why the RPC's getLatestBlockhash returns a last valid block height — the block height the blockhash came from, plus 150. The count is in block height, not slots: a skipped slot produces no block and does not age a blockhash, so wallets track expiry by height even though the epoch schedule (Tool 12) runs on slots. Because the age is 0-indexed, a blockhash can land at 151 distinct block heights — its own height through its last valid height, inclusive — and one block past that it is expired forever: the transaction must be rebuilt and re-signed on a fresh blockhash. The headline blockhash, from block height 250,000,000, seen at a current height of 250,000,120, is 120 blocks old with a last valid height of 250,000,150 — 30 blocks remaining. A durable-nonce transaction is the exception — its stored nonce value does not age this way — and is not modelled here, and no wall-clock time is quoted: the window is counted in produced blocks, never seconds. An educational model of blockhash expiry, not a live cluster reading. Not financial advice.

An Anchor program never dispatches on a name string: every instruction call's data begins with an 8-byte discriminator — the first 8 bytes of the SHA-256 hash of global:<name>, where the name is the Rust function name in snake_case, exactly as declared — and every Anchor account's data begins with the same construction over account:<name>, the Rust struct name in PascalCase (Tool 22 budgets those 8 bytes at the front of every account). The headline instruction, initialize — the first instruction of nearly every Anchor program — hashes to afaf6d1f0d989bed, the byte list [175, 175, 109, 31, 13, 152, 155, 237]. The hash is case-sensitive: Initialize is a different discriminator, so renaming or recasing a function or struct silently changes it and breaks every existing client and account — the discriminator is part of a program's public interface, not an internal detail. Only the default hash-derived discriminator is modelled: newer Anchor versions let a program set a custom discriminator override instead, and those programs do not use this hash. An educational model of Anchor's default discriminator, not a live program reading. Not financial advice.

64. Account reallocation planner — growing an account 10,240 bytes at a time

A program can resize its own account with realloc, but the runtime caps each single growth at 10,240 bytes (10 KiB) — growing by more takes that many separate realloc calls — while shrinking in one call is uncapped, and no account may exceed 10 MiB (10,485,760 bytes) of data. The account must stay rent-exempt at its new length, so growth needs a top-up of exactly the rent difference (Tool 3's figures at the two lengths, 6,960 lamports per added byte), and shrinking releases the same figure back. Growing a 1,024-byte account to 100,000 bytes is 98,976 bytes of growth: 10 realloc calls (the last grows the remaining 6,816 bytes) and a top-up of 688,872,960 lamports (0.68887296 SOL). An educational model of account reallocation, not a live account reading. Not financial advice.

65. Durable nonce planner — pre-signing transactions that do not expire in 150 blocks

A normal transaction dies 150 blocks after its blockhash (Tool 62). A durable nonce transaction signs against the blockhash stored in a nonce account instead, and its first instruction must be AdvanceNonceAccount — which replaces the stored value the moment the transaction lands, so each stored value backs exactly one transaction and the transaction carries no block-height expiry until the nonce advances. The account's state is 80 bytes and must be rent-exempt, so each account locks 1,447,680 lamports (Tool 3's figure for 80 bytes) — locked, not spent, and reclaimed when the account is closed. Pre-signing 10 transactions that may land in any order takes 10 nonce accounts, locking 14,476,800 lamports (0.0144768 SOL); an ordered chain can share one account, because each advance produces exactly the value the next transaction signed against, but it must then land strictly in signing order. The nonce authority must sign the advance, and an account's stored value can also be advanced by hand, which invalidates anything signed against the old value. An educational model of durable-nonce planning, not a live account reading. Not financial advice.

66. Token-2022 mint extension space planner — what each extension adds to a mint

A Token-2022 mint keeps the classic 82-byte mint layout, and every extension a builder enables is appended after it as a TLV record — a 2-byte type and a 2-byte length in front of the extension's own fixed-size data — plus a single account-type byte ahead of the first record. So a mint carrying a transfer fee config (108 bytes of data) and a metadata pointer (64 bytes) is 82 + 1 + 112 + 68 = 263 bytes, funded rent-exempt at 2,721,360 lamports (Tool 3's figure at that size). With no extensions the account is exactly 82 bytes and carries no marker byte at all. One trap is built into the length calculation: if a combination would total exactly 355 bytes — the length of a multisig account — 2 bytes are added (a transfer fee config with a token group and a group member lands there and ships at 357), so no extended account can be mistaken for a multisig by length alone. The same extension picked twice counts once. The variable-length token metadata extension is not modelled — its size depends on the metadata packed into it — and token-account extensions are a separate list. An educational model of Token-2022 mint sizing, not a live mint reading. Not financial advice.

67. Token-2022 token-account extension space planner — what each extension adds to a token account

The token-account side of Tool 66. A Token-2022 token account keeps the classic 165-byte token-account layout, and every account extension is appended after it as a TLV record — a 2-byte type and a 2-byte length in front of the extension's own data — plus a single account-type byte ahead of the first record. So an account carrying a transfer fee amount record (8 bytes of data) and an immutable owner record (0 bytes) is 165 + 1 + 12 + 4 = 182 bytes, funded rent-exempt at 2,157,600 lamports (Tool 3's figure at that size). The familiar 2,039,280-lamport token-account figure (Tools 27 and 58) is the no-extensions case, exactly 165 bytes with no marker byte at all. Several records are not the account owner's choice: a transfer-fee mint puts the transfer fee amount record — where the withheld fees sit (Tool 13) — on every one of its token accounts, so each of its accounts is at least 178 bytes at 2,129,760 lamports even when the owner picks nothing, and confidential-transfer, non-transferable, transfer-hook and pausable mints do the same with their account records; immutable owner, memo transfer and CPI guard are the owner's own picks. The big one is confidential transfer: 295 bytes of data make a 465-byte account at 4,127,280 lamports. Zero-data extensions still cost their 4-byte header — an immutable-owner account alone is 170 bytes. And the multisig padding of Tool 66 can never trigger here: without the confidential record the largest possible total is 205 bytes, with it the smallest is 465, so no combination of the eight account extensions lands on 355. The same extension picked twice counts once. An educational model of Token-2022 token-account sizing, not a live account reading. Not financial advice.

68. Compute budget planner — the limit a transaction is given, and the one it should ask for

Every transaction runs against a compute-unit limit. Never set one and the runtime gives the transaction 200,000 compute units per instruction, capped at the transaction maximum of 1,400,000 — so three instructions get 600,000 by default. The limit matters twice. Execution fails the moment usage passes it, so a transaction whose real usage — measured by simulating it — exceeds the default dies unless it asks for more with a SetComputeUnitLimit instruction. And the prioritization fee (Tool 5) is charged on the limit requested, not the compute used, so an oversized limit is paid for in full: at 10,000 micro-lamports per unit, the 1,400,000 maximum costs 14,000 lamports whether the transaction uses it or not. The planner's headline: 3 instructions measuring 412,000 compute units used, given 20% headroom, should request 494,400 — a priority fee of 4,944 lamports at that price, against 6,000 charged on the 600,000 default. Too little headroom is the symmetric failure: usage that varies between simulations can pass the measured figure, which is why the recommendation grosses usage up rather than requesting it exactly. And some work fits in no transaction at all: usage above 1,400,000 must be split, and headroom that would push a near-maximum transaction past the cap is capped there and flagged, leaving no spare room. An educational model of compute budgeting, not a live simulation. Not financial advice.

69. Interest-bearing token display calculator — the balance that grows without a single token moving

A Token-2022 mint with the interest-bearing extension never mints its interest. The raw amount in your token account stays exactly what was deposited, and what grows is the displayed amount, computed when a wallet reads the balance: displayed = raw × erate × time, compounded continuously. The rate is a signed count of basis points per year stored on the mint — negative rates are representable and shrink the display — and the time runs against a year of 365.24 days (31,556,736 seconds), so 365 days is not quite a full year: at 500 bps, a raw 10,000 displays as 10,512.365573169 after 365 days. When the rate authority changes the rate, the mint stores a pre-update average rate for the stretch before the change and applies the new rate only from the change onward, so the total factor is the product of the two stretches' exponentials — the headline below's second example accrues at an average 300 bps for 200 days, then 600 bps for 100. The grown figure is truncated at the smallest unit, never rounded, and a transfer still moves raw amounts: the growth exists only in the conversion, which is the reference client SDK's own formula, verified case-for-case before this tool shipped. An educational model of the interest-bearing display conversion, not a live mint reading. Not financial advice.

70. Scaled UI amount display calculator — the balance a multiplier rewrites

A Token-2022 mint with the scaled UI amount extension carries a multiplier on the mint, and wallets display raw × multiplier instead of the raw amount — the raw balance in the account never changes, which is how these mints model rebasing tokens and stock splits: a 2-for-1 split is the multiplier doubling, not a mint to every holder. The multiplier authority can also schedule a new multiplier with an effective timestamp: reads before it use the stored multiplier, reads at or after it use the new one, a >= comparison against the clock. Below, a raw 10,000 at a multiplier of 2 displays as 20,000, and from timestamp 1767225600 the scheduled multiplier of 3 displays the same raw balance as 30,000 — no transfer, mint or burn happened in between. Both conversions truncate at the smallest unit. The way back matters as much: a user entering a displayed amount to send is converted to raw by dividing by the multiplier, and because the reference conversion does that division in floating point it can land one smallest unit short — 63,146 displayed at a multiplier of 2 converts back to 31,572.999999999 raw, not the exact-decimal 31,573 — which is why apps are told to round down, leave the dust, and send the full raw balance on “max”. The program accepts only a positive, normal float as a multiplier. An educational model of the scaled-UI display conversion, not a live mint reading. Not financial advice.

71. LP fee earnings estimator — what your share of a pool's fees comes to

Every swap through a constant-product pool pays the pool's fee tier — basis points of the trade — and that fee stays in the pool for its liquidity providers, shared pro-rata: the pool collects volume × fee tier, and a position earns the slice its deposit is of the pool's total liquidity. Below, a pool trading 1,000,000 a day at a 25 bps tier collects 2,500 a day; a deposit of 10,000 into a pool holding 10,000,000 is a 0.1% share, so it earns 2.5 a day — 75 over 30 days. Annualised naively that is a 9.125% APR; compounded daily — each day's earnings joining the position and earning themselves — it is about 9.553% APY, and the gap between those two numbers is exactly why a quoted yield means nothing until you ask which one it is. Both floors land at the smallest unit, so a dust share can floor a whole day's earnings to zero while the pool still collects. The volume, tier and pool size are held frozen — an annualisation of one day, never a forecast — impermanent loss (Tools 4 and 60) is not deducted, and a concentrated position earns only while the price is inside its range (its already-accrued fees are Tool 25's accounting, not this estimate). An educational model of LP fee earnings, not a live pool quote. Not financial advice.

72. Prediction-market outcome shares — a price that is a probability

A binary prediction market trades two outcome shares, YES and NO, anchored to the collateral by a complete-set rule: paying exactly 1 unit of collateral mints one YES plus one NO, and one of each redeems for exactly 1, whichever side wins. That anchor makes a share price an implied probability — a YES at 0.62 prices a 62% chance, because the share pays exactly 1 if YES happens and 0 if it does not — and it makes the two prices of a consistent market sum to exactly 1. Below, YES at 0.62 and NO at 0.41 sum to 1.03, 300 bps rich: minting complete sets for 1 and selling both sides returns 1.03 a set, so a spend of 620 locked as collateral comes back as 638.6, a gross edge of 18.6 before fees. Prices summing under 1 run the same rule backwards — buy both sides for the sum, redeem for 1. The same spend pointed at YES alone buys 1,000 shares (620 / 0.62): they pay 1,000 if YES happens, a profit of 380, and exactly 0 if it does not, which is why the price is also the trade's break-even probability. A price of 0 or 1 is a settled market, not a tradeable one, and is rejected here. Market fees, spread and price movement while trading are not modelled. An educational model of prediction-market outcome shares, not a live market quote. Not financial advice.

Getting started building on Solana

  1. Install the Solana CLI from the official installation guide, then point it at devnet: solana config set --url devnet.
  2. Make a devnet wallet and fund it free: solana-keygen new, then solana airdrop 2. Devnet SOL has no value — it exists so you can break things safely.
  3. Learn the account model first (Tool 3 above): every program you write allocates accounts, and rent-exempt minimums decide how much SOL each one locks up.
  4. Price a transaction before you send it (Tool 5 above): 5,000 lamports per signature plus a priority fee charged on the compute-unit limit you request — simulate, then set the limit close to real usage instead of overpaying for headroom you never use.
  5. Convert token amounts with the mint's own decimals (Tool 6 above): token accounts store raw integers, and the same raw number is a different UI amount under every mint — read the decimals off the mint before you move an amount between code and UI.
  6. Check every address before you trust it (Tool 7 above): an address is 32 bytes in base58 — a paste with stray or missing characters decodes to the wrong length. Format is only the first check; still compare the full address character-for-character with its source.
  7. Model staking before you delegate (Tool 8 above): rewards arrive once per epoch (about 2 days), commission comes off the reward — not the stake — and credited rewards compound. Compare validators on commission and reliability, never on a headline yield alone; the yield you model is an assumption, not a promise.
  8. Read a payment link before you pay it (Tool 9 above): a Solana Pay URL names the recipient, the amount in user units and the asset — an empty amount means the wallet asks, a memo is public on-chain forever, and a well-formed link can still point at the wrong recipient, so inspect it and compare the recipient in full.
  9. Place concentrated liquidity by ticks, not vibes (Tool 10 above): on Raydium CLMM and Orca Whirlpools a price is a tick — price = 1.0001^tick — a position earns only while the current tick is inside its range, and its endpoints must align to the pool's tick spacing. A narrow range earns more per unit while it lasts and goes inactive sooner; check both before you open one.
  10. Set slippage deliberately on every swap (Tool 11 above): the tolerance becomes a signed bound — minimum received = expected out × (1 − tolerance), floored, or maximum input = expected in × (1 + tolerance) — and the program aborts rather than fill past it. Too tight and normal price movement fails the swap (you still pay the fee); too loose and a bad fill, or a sandwich, passes. Basis points are the unit: 50 bps = 0.5%.
  11. Never compute an epoch as slot ÷ 432,000 (Tool 12 above): the chain warmed up — epochs doubled from 32 slots until the first normal epoch, 14, at slot 524,256 — so the naive division is a dozen-plus epochs behind, forever. Epoch boundaries are when staking rewards land and leader schedules rotate; use the runtime's EpochSchedule (or this converter), and never convert slots to wall-clock time with a fixed slot duration — the target has changed repeatedly.
  12. Check whether a token withholds a transfer fee (Tool 13 above): a Token-2022 mint can carry a transfer fee — the sender is debited in full, the recipient is credited the amount minus ceil(amount × rate ÷ 10,000), capped at the mint's maximum fee, and the withheld fee sits in the recipient's token account until harvested. A quote or invoice in such a token that ignores the fee under-delivers on every payment; gross up when an exact received amount matters, and remember the classic SPL Token program has no transfer fees at all.
  13. Understand what a sandwich does to a loose tolerance (Tool 14 above): an attacker front-runs a visible swap, lets it fill worse, and back-runs into the price move — the profit is the victim's loss. The slippage bound is the defence: tight enough and the victim's transaction aborts instead of filling (the attacker then loses the round-trip unwinding alone); loose enough and the bad fill passes its own protection. Private routing keeps a swap out of the public flow the attack reads.
  14. Price a liquid staking token by its rate, not its balance (Tool 15 above): an LST does not rebase — rewards grow the pool's total SOL while the supply stands still, so the SOL-per-token rate rises and the same token amount redeems for more SOL over time. A rate below 1, or a deposit that mints at a stale rate, is a red flag; and a dust deposit can floor to zero tokens, so never deposit an amount whose minted figure you have not checked.
  15. Derive program addresses from seeds, never store a key for them (Tool 16 above): a PDA is SHA-256(seeds ‖ bump ‖ program ID ‖ "ProgramDerivedAddress") kept only when the hash lands off the ed25519 curve — a result on the curve could have a private key and is rejected, which is why derivation scans bump 255 downward for the first off-curve hash. At most 16 seeds, each at most 32 bytes; seeds are raw bytes (text means its UTF-8 encoding; a pubkey seed is its 32 bytes — an associated token account is exactly this derivation). A PDA has no private key: only its program can sign for it, with those seeds and that bump.
  16. Size a concentrated position from its liquidity, not from token amounts (Tool 17 above): a CLMM position is L plus a tick range, and the amounts follow — inside the range it holds both assets in the proportion the current tick sets, below the range only token0, above it only token1, sliding continuously as the price crosses. At tick 0 over −600…600, L = 1,000,000 holds about 29,553 of each. The amounts are principal only: earned fees accrue separately, and every price move re-splits the position toward the asset being sold — that drift is the exposure behind impermanent loss.
  17. Deposit into a pool at its ratio, not yours (Tool 18 above): a constant-product pool mints LP tokens from the smaller side's contribution — supply × min(depositA ÷ reserveA, depositB ÷ reserveB) — and only the ratio-matching part of a deposit is used, so an off-ratio deposit wastes the excess unless the interface returns it. The first deposit is the exception: into an empty pool it sets the starting price and mints sqrt(depositA × depositB). Your share afterwards is minted ÷ new supply, and burning it later returns that share of whatever the pool holds then — not what you put in.
  18. Read a vesting schedule by its cliff, not its accrual (Tool 19 above): an allocation can accrue linearly from day one yet be claimable for exactly 0 until the cliff month, when the whole accrued share releases at once - a 1,000,000-token allocation on a 12-month cliff over 48 months shows 125,000 accrued and 0 claimable at month 6, then 250,000 released at the cliff. Schedules differ: some start the linear accrual at the cliff instead of gating it, and real contracts run on timestamps, not whole months - read the terms before valuing any locked allocation.
  19. Judge a borrow by its health factor, not its LTV alone (Tool 20 above): a lending position is liquidatable when (collateral value x liquidation threshold) / debt falls below 1 - 10 tokens at 100 against 500 of debt at an 80% threshold has a health factor of 1.6 and a liquidation price of 62.5. The max LTV is the borrowing limit, the threshold is the liquidation line, and the gap between them is the safety buffer; interest accrual moves a real health factor even at a flat price.
  20. Budget a transaction's bytes like its fees (Tool 21 above): a transaction must fit one 1,232-byte packet - a plain SOL transfer is exactly 215 bytes (65 of signatures, 97 of static keys, 32 of blockhash, the rest header and instruction). Lookup tables shrink big transactions because a table account costs a 1-byte index instead of a 32-byte key, but the table reference costs 32-plus bytes first: loading one account through a table makes a transaction bigger, loading many makes it much smaller.
  21. Size every account before you allocate it (Tool 22 above): an Anchor account is 8 discriminator bytes plus its Borsh field bytes, packed with no padding — Pubkey 32, u64 8, bool 1, a String 4 plus its maximum length — and the total is exactly the rent-exempt deposit it locks: a vault struct of Pubkey + u64 + bool + String(32) is 85 bytes and 1,482,480 lamports. Over-allocate and the waste is permanent; under-allocate and the first write that outgrows the space fails.
  22. Time a stake's warmup or cooldown by the queue, not the calendar (Tool 24 above): stake changes state only at epoch boundaries, and per epoch at most 9% of the cluster's effective stake may activate or deactivate in total (900 bps; it was 25% before the rate reduction), shared pro-rata by everything moving — your epoch change is your portion ÷ the total moving × that cap, floored, with a 1-lamport minimum. A stake small against the cluster is fully effective after one epoch; whale-sized moves and crowded exits take several, which is why an unstake can still be draining after the epoch rolls.
  23. Read a CLMM position's fees from its counters, not a balance (Tool 25 above): uncollected fees are floor((fee growth inside now − at last touch) × liquidity ÷ 264) plus what an earlier touch already settled as owed — and the subtraction wraps modulo 2128, so a counter lower than its stored value is the u128 wrapping, not negative fees. Touching a position settles and re-stores the counter, which is why "since the last touch" is the only span the growth delta ever describes.
  24. Price dilution from the schedule, not from headlines (Tool 26 above): new SOL is issued at max(1.5%, 8% × 0.85years) — at year 5 that is 3.5496%, and the 1.5% terminal floor arrives at year ≈10.30, so issuance never stops, it just slows to the floor. The rate glides per epoch (the year is fractional on-chain), and a governance-adopted doubling of the disinflation rate to 30% is not a live parameter until the validator implements it — model it at 3,000 bps, don't quote it as current.
  25. Budget a compressed NFT collection by its tree, not per asset (Tool 28 above): a cNFT collection's on-chain cost is one concurrent Merkle tree account — 56 bytes of header, 64 fixed, a changelog ring of buffer × (40 + 32 × depth) bytes, 32 × depth of rightmost proof, plus any canopy — and the ring is almost all of it: a depth-14, buffer-64 tree is 31,800 bytes and 222,218,880 lamports of rent for 16,384 assets, of which 31,232 bytes are changelog. Depth sets capacity (2depth), the buffer (a power of two) sets how many concurrent updates the tree absorbs, and a canopy trades more rent up front for fewer proof hashes on every later transfer.
  26. Derive a seeded address when a signer — not a program — needs many accounts (Tool 29 above): a seeded address is SHA-256(base ‖ seed ‖ owner) — no marker, no bump, no curve check, so an on-curve result is valid (the hub's donation address with seed 0 under the Stake program derives 4z22cSCVREYe519iEet4xdqBSrTDx9muhtRR3fcRxpod). Reach for a PDA (Tool 16) when a program must sign; reach for a seed when a base keypair creates a family of accounts, one seed per member, and signs their creation itself. The seed is a single string of at most 32 UTF-8 bytes, used byte-for-byte.
  27. Read a token's valuation by which supply it multiplies (Tool 30 above): market cap is price × circulating supply, FDV is price × max supply, and the multiple between them is max ÷ circulating — the price cancels, so it is pure dilution still to come. At a price of 2.5 with 400,000,000 circulating of a 1,000,000,000 max, the market cap is 1,000,000,000 and the FDV 2,500,000,000: only 40% of the supply trades today, and the FDV assumes today's price survives the rest arriving.
  28. Plan a lookup table by its account, not just its savings (Tool 31 above): a table is an account of 56 metadata bytes + 32 per stored address (256 maximum, because the indexes are u8) with a rent-exempt deposit to match — 2,104 bytes and 15,534,720 lamports for 64 addresses — and its per-transaction saving is the table reference (32-byte key, a list byte and two shortvec counts) plus 1 byte per loaded address against 32 bytes inline: one loaded address costs 4 bytes MORE through a table, twenty cost 55 bytes instead of 640. Deactivating and closing a table to reclaim the deposit waits out a cooldown first.
  29. Read an oracle price as a band, never a point (Tool 32 above): a Pyth-style feed publishes price ± conf at one exponent, conf being roughly one standard deviation — so judge a price by its band width in basis points of the price (150.00 ± 0.075 is 5 bps and passes a 1% guard; 67,890 ± 680 is 100.16 bps and fails that same guard), value collateral at the band's low end, and check staleness separately before trusting any of it.
  30. Price a launchpad token off its virtual reserves, not its vault (Tool 33 above): a bonding curve's price is virtual SOL ÷ virtual token on a constant-product curve — the virtual SOL is a pricing offset, while the SOL the curve actually holds is the real raised figure that counts toward graduation. Every buy moves the price along the curve (3 SOL into the headline curve receives 90,081,892.62966333 tokens and lifts the price 20.78%), and at graduation the real SOL and unsold tokens migrate to an AMM at a continuous price but with the virtual depth left behind.
  31. Price the funding before the leverage (Tool 34 above): a perpetual charges (or pays) funding every interval — payment = notional × rate × time, longs paying shorts at a positive rate and shorts paying longs at a negative one. A 100-unit long at 150 pays 1.5 per hour at just 1 bp per hour — 36 over a day — and that rate annualises to 87.6%, so a funding rate that looks like a rounding error is a real carry cost on any hold longer than a scalp; the rate resets every interval, which is why the annualised figure is a quote, never a forecast.
  32. Read a lending rate off its curve, not its headline (Tool 35 above): a borrow rate is a kinked function of utilization — borrowed ÷ supplied — climbing gently to the optimal utilization, then steeply past it, and suppliers earn the borrow rate × utilization × (1 − reserve factor). A market at exactly its 80% kink with a 2% base and 4% first slope charges 6% and pays suppliers 4.32%; the same market at 90% charges 36%. A quoted supply yield is only as durable as the utilization under it, and every parameter is governance-set, so check the curve before trusting either rate.
  33. Judge a perp by its liquidation price, not its leverage alone (Tool 36 above): equity = margin + PnL is liquidated when it falls to the maintenance margin — a venue-set rate times the current notional — so a 10x long at a 500 bps maintenance rate liquidates on a fall of only about 5.26%, while equity still remains; the same leverage multiplies your return on margin exactly, in both directions. Funding (Tool 34) is the carry on top of this, and no leverage at or below 1x has a liquidation price at all.
  34. Stable pools price off an invariant, not x × y = k (Tool 37 above): a stableswap pool's amplification coefficient holds the price near 1:1 while the pool stays balanced — a balanced 1,000,000 / 1,000,000 pool at A = 100 returns a 10,000 trade at 9,999.502463058, where constant-product returns 9,900 — but unbalance the pool and the same curve bends back toward constant-product pricing. The peg is defended by balanced liquidity, never guaranteed by the formula.
  35. Install Anchor from anchor-lang.com and run anchor init: you get a Rust program, an IDL and TypeScript tests in one scaffold.
  36. Read a real swap before writing one: run Tool 2 with a big amount in and watch price impact grow — that curve is what every AMM on Solana prices against.
  37. Test on devnet, then localnet: anchor test spins up a local validator; deploy to devnet only when tests pass. Never learn on mainnet with real funds.
  38. Ship and catalogue it: the full checklist lives in the getting-started guide in the repo.
